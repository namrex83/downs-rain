The Bureau of Meteorology's Toowoomba (Mt Kynoch) radar, 128, 256 or 512 km range, the last 90 minutes of pictures (every 5 minutes, or less often if BOM makes them less often). After the latest picture, the loop shows a prediction for the next 90 minutes: the latest rain slid along at the speed and direction it has been moving (measured from the radar about once an hour). It can't show storms growing, weakening, turning or new ones forming. 128 km has the most detail close to Toowoomba; 512 km shows weather coming from further away, in less detail. Zoom out to see a whole range. Colours are BOM's rain-rate scale; this is the live radar, before any gauge checks, so for totals use the Rainfall tab. Radar can miss low rain far from Toowoomba and over-read hail. Radar images © Commonwealth of Australia, Bureau of Meteorology.

Long-range outlooks · Bureau of Meteorology

These open on the Bureau's website (the SAM graph on NOAA's). Outlooks give the chance of a wetter or drier, warmer or cooler than usual season, not amounts on particular days.

Rain chart: each model's running total at the pin (or Dalby), day by day, in mm, with the dashed median of all of them. GFS, ECMWF and ECMWF AI are the maps above; ACCESS-G (the Bureau's model), UK Met Office (UKMO), ICON (Germany), GEM (Canada) and JMA (Japan) are read for the spot from Open-Meteo (CC BY 4.0). Each line stops where its model run ends; when the lines bunch together the forecast is more certain.

Each forecast is the model's rain on its own grid of squares about 25 km across, smoothed for the map. It shows the broad pattern for the district, not differences between paddocks, and storm rain in particular can land well away from where the model puts it. Forecast days run 10am to 10am, the model's nearest times to 9am. Contains modified Copernicus/ECMWF data, © ECMWF, licensed under CC BY 4.0.

Wind 10 m above the ground, the model's average speed (gusts are the strongest bursts it expects between times, often half as strong again). Arrows point the way the wind is blowing. The model's grid squares are about 25 km across, so it shows the broad pattern, not gullies, ranges or storm outflow: check the wind on the spot before spraying or burning. Contains modified Copernicus/ECMWF data, © ECMWF, licensed under CC BY 4.0.

A weather-model forecast of the soil temperature 10 cm down, worked out from the model's layers above and below 10 cm. It's for bare, average soil on grid squares about 25 km across, so stubble, soil moisture, soil colour and aspect can put your paddock a few degrees off. The tap readout checks the usual cotton planting guide: 14°C or more at 10 cm at 8am for at least 3 days in a row, and not about to turn cold. Check with a soil thermometer or probe before planting. Not measured readings: these will later be corrected against CSD soil probes, the way the rain maps are corrected against gauges.

Air temperature 2 m above the ground, as in a weather screen. Day max and min are picked from the model's 3-hourly times (6-hourly after day 6), so the real top and bottom of the day can be a degree or two past them. The model's grid squares are about 25 km across: frost hollows, creek flats and hill tops run colder or warmer than it shows, and on still, clear nights the ground can frost when the screen reads 2–4°C. Contains modified Copernicus/ECMWF data, © ECMWF, licensed under CC BY 4.0.

Severity: CAPE and shear combined (the square root of 2 × CAPE times the shear in m/s), a measure storm researchers use for how violent storms can get. Under 400 severe storms are unlikely; 400–1000 severe storms possible; 1000–1600 significant severe (large hail, damaging winds); 1600+ high-end. It is turned down where the air near the ground isn't unstable, and where a cap of stable air holds storms back (the inhibition, or "cap", in the tap readout; over about 50 J/kg is a real lid, as after a clear night). It shows how bad storms could be where they form, not where they will form. Lifted index: how much warmer than the surrounding air a surface parcel would be at about 5.5 km. Below 0 it keeps rising on its own; −4 to −6 is unstable, −6 or lower very unstable. CAPE: the energy available to updrafts; 1000+ J/kg is plenty for strong storms. Shear: change in wind between the ground and about 5.5 km; 30+ knots lets storms organise, 40+ suits supercells (large hail, damaging winds, rarely tornadoes). Helicity (0–3 km storm-relative helicity): how much the winds in the lowest 3 km turn and strengthen with height, which is what spins a storm's updraft. Worked out for the storm that rotates clockwise (the Southern Hemisphere supercell, usually moving left of the upper winds), so higher numbers mean more rotation. 100+ m²/s² supports supercells; 150–250+ strong rotation; high helicity with moist air near the ground is the tornado signal. Supercell (supercell composite): CAPE × helicity × shear in one number, the best single guide here to where supercells could form. Under 1 unlikely; 1–4 supercells possible; 4–8 likely if storms fire; 8+ a classic big supercell day. It is cut where a cap holds storms back. Combined shows where the ingredients overlap, with the severe areas graded by severity so the worst spots stand out. None of these says whether something will set storms off (a trough, front or sea breeze), so the BOM forecasts and warnings remain the ones to act on.

Radar rain rates come from Bureau of Meteorology images, added up every few minutes. Each 9am the total is scaled to match the BOM gauges, then nudged locally near each gauge.
